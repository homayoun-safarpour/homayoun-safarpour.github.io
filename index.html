How one run is checked

Six gates, one at a time.

Each gate answers a problem that regulators, standards bodies and researchers now report. Scroll to step through them.

  1. 1

    Behaviour

    Did the agent use only the reviewed tools, in order? A forbidden tool stops the run, whatever the score.

    Why it matters. Agents now finish software tasks that take an expert many hours, and METR says its longest measurements are already unreliable. Long runs need a check on what was actually done. METR, Task-Completion Time Horizons, 2026 ↗

  2. 2

    Policy

    Would Microsoft's policy engine have allowed every call? Anything not allowed by name is refused.

    Why it matters. OWASP's list of the top risks for agentic applications starts with goal hijack and tool misuse: an allowed tool used for the wrong purpose. OWASP Top 10 for Agentic Applications, 2026 ↗

  3. 3

    Completion

    Did the run finish without being cut off, and does it point to its proof?

    Why it matters. Anthropic reports that an automated reviewer of every change would have caught about a third of the bugs behind its past incidents. A claim of done needs proof someone else can check. Anthropic, When AI builds itself, 2026 ↗

  4. 4

    Hand-over

    Did it leave a complete record for the next person?

    Why it matters. The International AI Safety Report warns that agents act on their own, which makes it harder for people to step in before a failure causes harm. OpenAI reports that more than half of its successful long agent tasks still needed a person to step in. International AI Safety Report, 2026 ↗

  5. 5

    Judges

    Do the AI judges that scored the replies agree with each other?

    Why it matters. A study across 20 language tasks, peer-reviewed at ACL 2025, concludes that AI judges must be checked against human judgments before they are trusted. Another ACL 2025 paper found that apologetic wording alone can swing a safety judge's preference by up to 98 %. A judge can sound sure and still be steered. ACL 2025, LLMs instead of Human Judges? ↗ ACL 2025, Safer or Luckier? ↗

  6. 6

    Red team

    Did a security scan of the model find an attack that works?

    Why it matters. The same safety report notes that models more often tell a test from real use and find loopholes in evaluations, so a dangerous ability can pass unseen. International AI Safety Report, 2026 ↗

  7. =

    One verdict

    FAIL if any gate fails. A missing record is never a pass. Below, break the run yourself, then see the 90 sources behind each gate.

      Do the judges that scored the replies agree with each other?

      Inter-judge Fleiss kappa: 0.856 - almost perfect - check for a leaked cue or a degenerate label distribution

      What this gate means
      Checks
      Three judges each scored 10 replies three times. The kit measures how far the judges agree with each other (Fleiss kappa), how far each agrees with itself, and why they disagree.
      PASS means
      Kappa is 0.60 or higher, the kit's line for combining their scores.
      PASS does not prove
      That the judges are right. Agreement is not accuracy; that needs replies labelled by people. Ten replies show the method; they do not qualify a panel.
      Fails when
      Kappa is under 0.60. With the switch on it is 0.067, and the kit says not to combine the scores.
      Related to
      NIST AI RMF, MEASURE 2.13: checking the evaluation itself.

    Words used here
    Trace
    The recorded steps of one agent run: what it was asked, which tools it called, what it replied.
    Gate
    A check the run must pass before it is accepted. Each gate here is a separate tool.
    Rubric
    The reviewed list of what a run must and must not do.
    Pinned baseline
    The score of an approved run, frozen so later runs can be compared with it.
    Tolerance
    How far a new score may fall below the baseline before it fails. Here 0.05.
    Policy replay
    Running the recorded tool calls through a policy engine afterwards, to see what it would have allowed.
    Default deny
    Anything the policy does not allow by name is refused.
    Hand-over record
    What one agent or person leaves for the next: what was proved, what is pending, when to stop.
    Fleiss kappa
    How far several judges agree beyond chance. 1 is full agreement, 0 is what chance alone gives.
    Self-consistency
    How often a judge gives the same score when it rates the same reply again.
    Red team
    Attacking a system on purpose to find its weaknesses before someone else does.
    Fail-closed
    When evidence is missing or a tool breaks, the answer is not passed. Never passed.
    Leaked cue
    A hint of the expected answer in what the judges see. It makes agreement look better than it is.
    Degenerate labels
    Almost every reply gets the same label, so agreement figures say little.
    MISSING
    A required record was not there, so the tool could not run. The audit is then INCOMPLETE.
    sha256
    A fingerprint of a file. Change one byte and the fingerprint changes.

    Method

    How the tools fit together.

    Follow one change from a recorded run to a check another person can repeat.

    01 Record what happened

    Capture a run and retain its tool calls. Start with reproducible scenarios in the agent-eval-workbench.

    02 Compare against a known baseline

    Run trace-gate on the matching policy and baseline. Keep the verdict and the evidence that produced it.

    03 Check the judge

    Use the judge-reliability-kit to inspect agreement and the drift sentinel to monitor frozen examples.

    04 Decide what to do next

    Use the agent-loop-field-guide to record the next action, the check and when to stop.
